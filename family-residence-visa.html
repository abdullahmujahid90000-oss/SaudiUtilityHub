🏠 Family Residence Visa: Bringing Your Family Home to Saudi Arabia

Video calls only go so far. If you're ready to stop counting the days between holidays and have your spouse and children living with you here, this is the road map, from the first attested certificate to the day they collect their own iqamas.

🪪 Dependant iqama📄 Attestation first💰 Real costs, explained
Home › Family Residence Visa Guide

Families tell the same story: the hard part isn't Saudi Arabia, it's the paperwork that has to happen back home. Get that part right and everything after it is mostly waiting.

The short answer: if your iqama profession allows it, you can sponsor your spouse and children. You attest and translate your marriage and birth certificates, pay the family visa fee (widely reported as SAR 2,000), and submit the request through the government's recruitment and visa services (Istiqdam and the National Platform, my.gov.sa). Your family gets the visa stamped in their home country, flies in, completes biometrics, and receives a dependant iqama. From then on there's a SAR 400 monthly dependant fee for each family member.

Do You Qualify as a Sponsor?

Three things decide whether your family can join you:

  • Your iqama profession. This is the big one. Professional and skilled job titles usually qualify. Many labour-category titles do not, whatever the salary. If your title is wrong, ask HR to correct it through Qiwa before you start.
  • Your salary. A minimum income is widely reported, often around SAR 4,000 a month or more, but the authorities don't publish one official figure. Your salary in GOSI and Qiwa records should comfortably support a family.
  • A valid iqama with time left on it. Your family's residence hangs on yours.

There's also good news. Reports from October 2026 say skilled workers can now file their dependants' applications alongside their own work visa, so new hires don't always have to wait until after arrival to start. The core requirements above haven't changed.

The Paperwork That Makes or Breaks It

Get these ready before you open any portal:

  • Marriage certificate for your spouse
  • Birth certificates for each child, showing both parents' names
  • Passports for everyone, with plenty of validity left
  • Your degree certificate, if your profession requires it

Certificates normally need a chain of attestation: first the issuing authority and foreign ministry in your home country, then the Saudi embassy or consulate there, then the Ministry of Foreign Affairs here, followed by an Arabic translation. The exact chain differs by country. Ask the Saudi embassy in your home country before you pay any agent.

Step by Step: From Application to Iqama

  1. Attest and translate the certificates, as described above.
  2. Pay the family visa fee through SADAD. It is widely reported as SAR 2,000 for the family.
  3. Submit the request through my.gov.sa and the Istiqdam service, with your documents. Some cases still need an office visit.
  4. Visa issued. You receive the visa document and send it to your family.
  5. Stamping back home. Your family completes a medical check and biometrics, then gets the visa stamped through the Saudi embassy or its visa-service partner (Tasheer/VFS in many countries).
  6. Arrival. Buy health insurance, then complete biometrics at Jawazat.
  7. Dependant iqama issued. Pay the dependant fee and issue the iqamas through Absher. Each family member is now on your file.

    Mistakes That Cost Families Months

    • Starting with the visa, not the certificates. Attestation is the slow part, so do it first.
    • Name spellings that don't match. "Fatima" on the passport and "Fatimah" on the marriage certificate can stall everything.
    • Ignoring your iqama profession. No salary will make up for a job title that doesn't allow family.
    • Unpaid dependant fees. Jawazat links them to your own services, so unpaid fees can block your iqama renewal and exit re-entry visas.
    • Forgetting newborns. A baby born in Saudi Arabia must be registered and added to your iqama through Absher. Don't leave it until you need to travel.

    Already Here on a Visit Visa?

    Converting a visit visa to residence from inside the Kingdom has been allowed in some cases and not in others over the years, and current reports don't agree. Don't plan around it. The reliable route is to apply from scratch while your family is in their home country. If you're hoping to convert, ask Jawazat through Absher before your family's visit expires.

      Frequently Asked Questions

      Is a family residence visa permanent?
      No. Your family's iqamas are tied to yours and renewed along with it. If your own residency ends, theirs does too, so it lasts as long as you keep your job and iqama in order.
      How much is the family residence visa fee?
      The government visa fee is widely reported as SAR 2,000 for the family, paid through SADAD before you submit the request. Add attestation, translation, insurance and the SAR 400 monthly dependant fee for each family member.
      What salary do I need to sponsor my family?
      No single official figure is published. Many guides mention around SAR 4,000 a month or more. Your iqama profession matters more than salary: some labour-category job titles can't sponsor family at all.
      When does the SAR 400 dependant fee start?
      It's charged for each dependant on your iqama and is usually paid in advance when the iqama is issued or renewed. Many reports say new arrivals aren't charged for their first 90 days, but some families have been asked to pay at first issuance. Check the amount Absher shows.
      Can I convert my family's visit visa to residence?
      It has been possible in some periods and cases, but current reports conflict. The dependable route is a residence visa applied for while your family is in their home country.
      Which documents need attestation?
      Usually your marriage certificate and each child's birth certificate, sometimes also your degree. They go through your home country's authorities, the Saudi embassy there and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Saudi Arabia, followed by Arabic translation.
